# Chapter 9. Introduction to investing

This chapter is education about concepts. It does not recommend any investment, fund, broker, platform or app, and nothing here is a suggestion to buy or sell anything. Investing involves risk, including the loss of money you put in.

## What investing is

Saving keeps money for a use you expect soon, in places meant to hold their value. Investing puts money into assets whose value can rise or fall, such as shares in companies, bonds (loans to governments or companies), property or funds that hold a mix of these, in the hope of a return over a long period. The possibility of a higher return exists because of the possibility of a loss. Anyone who promises a high return with no risk is describing something that does not exist (see Chapter 11).

## Return comes from risk, with no guarantee

Higher expected returns usually require accepting larger and less predictable swings. "Expected" does not mean "promised": the actual outcome may be much better or worse, and a long-term average says nothing about the next year.

## Volatility and how losses work

Volatility is how much a value moves up and down. Losses are harder to recover than they look:

- A fall of 50% needs a rise of **100%** to get back: 1,000 CU becomes 500, and 500 x 2 = 1,000.
- A fall of 20% followed by a rise of 25% returns to the start: 1,000 x 0.80 = 800; 800 x 1.25 = 1,000.

This is why the time horizon matters (below) and why money needed soon should not be placed where a fall of this size is plausible.

## Time horizon

Time horizon is when you will need the money. Longer horizons give more time to recover from falls; shorter ones give less. A common planning idea is that money needed within a few years belongs in stable, accessible places, and that investing is for money you will not need for a long time. A year with a big fall near the date you need the money is a real danger known as sequence risk.

## Risk and return in practice: asset classes in general

| Type | What it is | Main risks |
|---|---|---|
| Cash and deposits | Money in bank accounts | Inflation; limits on deposit protection |
| Bonds | Loans to governments or companies | Rates, inflation, borrower default |
| Shares (equities) | Part-ownership of companies | Large swings; company failure |
| Property | Buildings and land | Illiquid, costs, local market |
| Funds | Pooled holdings of the above | Fees; inherits the risk of what it holds |

## Diversification

Diversification means spreading money across many holdings that do not all move together, so one failure does not decide the result. It lowers the risk of a single failure; it does not remove risk. In a broad market fall, many holdings fall at once.

**Concentration example (illustrative).** You hold 10,000 CU. If it is all in one company and the company's value falls 40%, you lose 4,000 CU. If it is split equally across ten unrelated holdings and one of them falls 40% while the other nine are unchanged, you lose 1,000 x 0.40 = 400 CU. Diversification limited the damage from one failure. It would not have helped if all ten fell.

## Funds, "index" and individual holdings: the generic difference

A **fund** pools many people's money to hold many assets. Some funds track a published list of companies (an index) and try to match it; others have a manager who tries to beat a benchmark. Individual securities (one company's shares or bonds) carry company-specific risk that a broad fund spreads out. Funds carry their own risks: fees, the risks of what they hold, and in some structures, the risk of the firm running them. This book does not say which kind to choose. If you are considering a product, read its official fact sheet or disclosure, check that the seller is authorised by your country's regulator, and understand every fee before you commit.

## Fees

Fees come in several forms: fund charges, platform or account fees, advice fees, trading costs and taxes. Chapter 8 shows that a 1 percentage point annual charge reduced a 30-year result by 27,641.85 CU on the assumed example. Ask for the total annual cost as a percentage and in currency terms.

## Retirement and tax-advantaged accounts, in general terms

Many countries offer accounts designed for long-term or retirement saving that give tax benefits or employer contributions, often with rules about when and how money can be withdrawn. The names, limits, penalties and eligibility are local and change over time. Before choosing, find your country's official explanation (the tax authority or pensions regulator), check whether your employer contributes, and read the withdrawal rules. Do not rely on a summary from a seller of financial products.

## Behavioural mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | Safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Chasing recent winners | Buy after a rise, sell after a fall | Decide rules before you invest |
| Panic selling | Locks in losses | Define in advance what you will do in a 30% fall |
| Overconfidence | Concentrated bets | Limit any single holding |
| Checking constantly | Stress, impulsive moves | Review on a calendar |
| Following social media | Hype, hidden promotions | Check source and incentives |
| Investing emergency money | Forced sales | Fund the cushion first |
| Ignoring fees | Quiet drag | Calculate total cost |

## A pre-investing checklist

- [ ] I have a starter emergency fund (Chapter 4).
- [ ] I have a plan for high-interest debt (Chapter 6).
- [ ] The money will not be needed for a long time.
- [ ] I can describe in one sentence what the product holds and how it makes money.
- [ ] I know every fee, in percent and in currency.
- [ ] The seller is authorised by my country's regulator (I checked the official register).
- [ ] I could live with a large fall without selling.
- [ ] I have read the official disclosure document.
- [ ] I have considered advice from a qualified, regulated adviser for my situation.

## Common mistakes and fixes

- **Investing before the cushion exists.** Build the cushion first.
- **Treating assumed returns as likely.** Use a range, including zero and negative years.
- **Buying what you cannot explain.** If you cannot explain it simply, wait.

# Chapter 10. Understanding financial risk

## Risk is more than market movement

| Risk | What it is | Reduce it by |
|---|---|---|
| Income risk | Job or client loss | Emergency fund, skills, several clients |
| Market risk | Investment values falling | Diversification, time horizon |
| Inflation risk | Prices rising faster than your money | Not holding long-term money only in cash |
| Interest-rate risk | Variable payments rising | Fixed rates, margin in budget |
| Credit and debt risk | Inability to repay | Low fixed costs, avoid stacked debt |
| Liquidity risk | Cannot turn an asset into cash quickly | Hold accessible savings |
| Concentration risk | Too much in one place (one employer, asset, country) | Spread |
| Health and life risk | Illness, injury, death | Insurance, will, plan |
| Fraud risk | Being deceived | Chapter 11 |
| Longevity risk | Outliving savings | Long-term planning |

## Risk tolerance versus risk capacity

**Tolerance** is how much risk you are willing to take; it is emotional. **Capacity** is how much risk you can afford to take; it is financial. They can differ. A person who feels relaxed about large swings but needs the money in two years has high tolerance and low capacity. A cautious person with decades ahead and a secure income has lower tolerance and higher capacity. Plan to the **lower** of the two, and revisit when circumstances change.

```
RISK SELF-CHECK
1. When will I need this money? ______ years
2. If it fell 30 percent, could I avoid selling? Y / N
3. How stable is my income? (stable / variable / uncertain)
4. Would a loss change my ability to pay essentials? Y / N
5. Do I have a cushion that covers 3+ months? Y / N
Any "N" or "uncertain" in questions 2-5 = reduce risk or wait.
```

## Insurance basics

Insurance transfers a rare, expensive loss to a company in return for a regular premium. Its purpose is to cover losses you could not absorb, not small ones you could.

**Key terms:**

- **Premium:** the regular price of cover.
- **Excess or deductible:** the amount you pay yourself before the insurer pays.
- **Limit:** the most the insurer will pay.
- **Exclusions:** situations not covered. Read them.

**Example of the trade-off (illustrative).** Cover A costs 40 CU a month (480 a year) with a 200 CU excess. Cover B costs 25 CU a month (300 a year) with a 800 CU excess. B saves 180 CU a year (480 - 300), but if you claim, you pay 600 CU more of the cost (800 - 200). B is reasonable only if you hold the higher excess in your emergency fund.

**Types people commonly consider (availability and rules vary by country):** health, income protection or disability, life (if others depend on your income), home or renters, vehicle, and liability. Only buy cover you understand, from an insurer authorised in your country, and compare terms, not only price.

## Concentration

Concentration includes holding mostly your employer's shares, relying on one client for most of your income, or having all savings in one place. Check each year: what single event would cause the largest damage, and what can I do about it?

# Chapter 11. Avoiding financial scams

## Why scams work

Scammers rely on pressure, trust and urgency rather than technology. Anyone can be fooled, particularly when stressed or when a pitch matches a real need. The defence is a fixed set of rules you apply before you pay or share information, regardless of how convincing the message is.

## Common patterns

The patterns below are consistent with the guidance on the US Federal Trade Commission's consumer site (pages opened 2026-11-07; see Appendix D). Other countries' agencies publish similar warnings.

**Impersonation.** The contact claims to be a government agency, bank, utility, tech company or charity. Caller ID and email names can be faked. The FTC's guidance notes that the agency itself will never threaten you or tell you to move money to protect it.

**A problem or a prize.** You owe money, have a virus, or have won something but must pay a fee first.

**Pressure to act now.** Threats of arrest, lawsuits or account closure; instructions to stay on the phone.

**Unusual payment methods.** Gift cards, wire transfers, payment apps and cryptocurrency. Cryptocurrency payments usually cannot be reversed and typically lack the dispute protection that credit cards offer.

**Advance-fee scams.** You are asked to pay a fee upfront to receive a loan, prize, job or inheritance. The fee is the scam.

**Overpayment or fake cheque.** Someone sends a cheque for more than owed and asks you to send back the difference before the cheque is found to be invalid.

**Romance scams.** The scammer builds an online relationship, avoids meeting in person (they claim to live abroad or work on a rig, in the military or for an international organisation), then asks for money for medical bills, travel, visas or crypto investing.

**Crypto and "guaranteed return" schemes.** The FTC's crypto guidance lists these red flags: guaranteed or large returns (nobody can guarantee profits; "zero risk" or "low risk" claims are false for a volatile asset), free-money offers, faked celebrity endorsements and testimonials, vague explanations, and upfront fees. A common version: a stranger offers to grow your money if you move crypto to their website, which looks real but is fake, and you cannot withdraw.

**Pig-butchering-type approaches.** A long, friendly online conversation that gradually moves to an "investment" platform. Early small "profits" on a fake dashboard can encourage larger deposits, and withdrawals are then blocked or require more fees. This combines the romance and fake-investment patterns above (the FTC pages describe both components; the term "pig butchering" itself is used in news and regulator coverage that this book did not open directly). The key signal: someone you met online who suggests a specific investment platform.

**Phishing.** Emails, texts or messages that mimic a real organisation and urge you to click a link or enter a password. Do not use the link; go to the organisation's own site, or call a number you looked up yourself.

## Scam-check checklist

Before sending money or information, answer these:

- [ ] Did they contact me first (unsolicited)?
- [ ] Do they claim to be someone official, or someone I have only met online?
- [ ] Are they pressuring me to act now or keep it secret?
- [ ] Do they want gift cards, a transfer, a payment app or crypto?
- [ ] Is there a promise of guaranteed, large or "risk-free" returns?
- [ ] Is there an upfront fee to receive money or a prize?
- [ ] Does the seller appear on my regulator's official register of authorised firms?
- [ ] Have I searched the name plus "scam" or "complaint"?
- [ ] Have I told a trusted person and waited at least one day?
- [ ] Would a legitimate business give me time to decide? (It would.)

One "yes" to the first six is a reason to stop; several is almost certainly a scam.

## Protect yourself in advance

- Use unique passwords and two-factor authentication on email and banking.
- Set low limits on payment apps and transfers where possible.
- Agree a family code word for emergency money requests.
- Never share one-time codes or remote-access to your device.
- Keep a list of official contact numbers for your bank, written from its card or site.

## If you have been scammed

Act quickly. The steps below follow the FTC's "what to do" guidance (US page opened 2026-11-07). In other countries the same principle applies: contact the payment provider first, then report to your national agency.

1. **Stop contact** with the scammer. Do not send more money to "recover" losses; that is a common second scam.
2. **Contact the payment provider immediately,** using the number on your card or in your official app:
   - bank transfer or payment app: ask for a reversal;
   - wire transfer: report it and ask for a refund;
   - credit or debit card: ask for a refund (chargeback);
   - gift card: contact the card issuer, and keep the card and receipt;
   - cryptocurrency: contact the exchange or ATM operator; recovery is often difficult.
3. **Protect accounts.** If you gave login details, change the password everywhere you used it and turn on two-factor authentication. If someone had access to your computer or phone, update security software, scan, and change passwords.
4. **If you gave identity details,** use your country's identity-theft recovery service. In the US that is IdentityTheft.gov.
5. **Report it.** In the US: ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Elsewhere, report to your national consumer or fraud-reporting agency and to your local police if your country requires it.
6. **Talk to someone.** Shame keeps people from reporting, and scammers rely on it.

## Common mistakes and fixes

- **Paying a "recovery" firm.** Anyone who contacts you offering to get your money back is likely running a second scam.
- **Believing screenshots of profits.** They are easy to fake.
- **Using the contact details in the message.** Use details you find independently.

# Chapter 12. Long-term financial planning

## Plan in layers

1. **Goals.** What do you want money to do, and when?
2. **Net worth.** Where do you stand now?
3. **Annual review.** Are you on track?
4. **Life events.** What will change the plan?
5. **Protection.** What if you cannot work, or die?

## Setting goals

Use time horizons:

| Horizon | Examples | Typical home for the money |
|---|---|---|
| Under 2 years | Holiday, laptop, course fees | Stable, accessible savings |
| 2 to 7 years | Deposit for a home, a car, a move | Mostly stable, any investing is a deliberate risk |
| 7+ years | Retirement, long-term wealth | Longer-horizon, with the risks in Chapter 9 |

Write each goal as a target amount and date (see the goal planner in Chapter 7).

## Net worth statement

Net worth is assets minus liabilities. Assets: cash, savings, investments, retirement accounts, valuables you could sell. Liabilities: all debts. Do not count assets you would not sell (a home you live in is counted by some people and excluded by others; choose one approach and keep it).

**Illustrative example (fictional):**

| Assets | CU | Liabilities | CU |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current account | 900 | Credit card | 1,300 |
| Emergency fund | 2,400 | Student loan | 6,800 |
| Retirement account | 5,200 | | |
| Laptop (resale value) | 400 | | |
| **Total assets** | **8,900** | **Total liabilities** | **8,100** |

Net worth = 8,900 - 8,100 = **800 CU**. A low number is common early on, especially with student debt; the direction over time matters more than the first figure.

```
NET WORTH STATEMENT                 Date: ________
ASSETS                       LIABILITIES
Cash/current: ______         Credit cards: ______
Emergency fund: ______       Loans: ______
Savings goals: ______        Student loans: ______
Investments: ______          Mortgage: ______
Retirement: ______           Other: ______
Other assets: ______
TOTAL ASSETS: ______         TOTAL LIABILITIES: ______
NET WORTH = assets - liabilities = ______
Change since last statement: ______
```

Update it on a fixed date each year (and a quick check every quarter). Use realistic resale values for property you own.

## The annual review

Once a year, ideally on a fixed date:

```
ANNUAL REVIEW
1. Update the net worth statement.
2. Compare to last year: up, flat or down? Why?
3. Savings rate last year: savings / net income = ____ %
4. Which goals were met? Which moved?
5. Debts: balances, rates, any chance to refinance on total cost?
6. Emergency fund: still the right number of months?
7. Insurance and beneficiaries: still right? (Chapter 10)
8. Investments: still suited to risk capacity and horizon? Fees?
9. Income: any change expected next year?
10. Pick three priorities for the next 12 months.
```

## Major life events

| Event | Consider |
|---|---|
| New job or pay change | Update budget, raise saving before spending |
| Moving in with someone or marriage | Shared accounts, who pays what, insurance, wills |
| Buying a home | Total cost, emergency fund after deposit, rate risk |
| Child | Cost, insurance, will and guardians |
| Loss of income | Emergency plan, contact lenders early |
| Illness or disability | Insurance terms, benefits available in your country |
| Divorce or bereavement | Professional legal and financial help |
| Inheritance or windfall | Pause; place in safe holding while you plan |
| Retirement | Withdrawal strategy, healthcare, longevity |

## Estate basics at a high level

These rules differ widely by country; take local legal advice.

- **A will** (or the equivalent) says who receives your property. Without one, local law decides.
- **Beneficiary designations** on retirement accounts, insurance and some bank accounts may override a will. Check they are current.
- **Power of attorney** (or equivalent) lets a chosen person manage your affairs if you cannot.
- **A list of accounts and contacts** in a safe place helps your family; do not store passwords in plain text.
- **Guardians** for children are usually named in a will.

## Common mistakes and fixes

- **Never doing the net worth number:** it takes 20 minutes a year.
- **Setting goals with no dates.** Add a date and a monthly amount.
- **Ignoring beneficiary forms.** Old designations can send money to the wrong person.
- **Letting lifestyle rise with every raise:** commit part of each raise to savings before it reaches your spending.

# Conclusion

A money system is a short list of habits that run without much thought: see your numbers, give each unit a job, protect against shocks, pay off expensive debt in a chosen order, save automatically, and invest cautiously with money you will not need. None of it needs a high income, and none of it is guaranteed to deliver a particular result; it improves your odds of staying in control.

Do this in order. Each week should take about one to two hours.

### Month 1: See and stabilise

**Week 1: Starting line.**
- [ ] Complete the starting statement (Chapter 1).

**Week 2: Track.**
- [ ] Build the income and expense tracker (Chapter 2).

**Week 3: Budget.**
- [ ] Choose your net income baseline (use the lowest-six method if irregular).
- [ ] Complete the monthly budget worksheet (Chapter 3) with a buffer line.
- [ ] Set up tax set-aside if you are self-employed.

**Week 4: Automate and protect.**
- [ ] Open a separate account for the emergency fund.
- [ ] Set a payday transfer, even a small one.
- [ ] Fill in the scam checklist's protective steps: password manager, two-factor on email and banking.

### Month 2: Cushion and debt

**Week 5: Emergency fund.**
- [ ] Calculate your target (Chapter 4) and your starter fund.
- [ ] Add one-time money (a refund, a sale of unused goods) if available.

**Week 6: Debt strategy.**
- [ ] Update APRs from statements.
- [ ] Run the repayment comparison (Chapter 6) and choose a strategy.
- [ ] Contact any lender you are behind with, or a free debt adviser.

**Week 7: Start the plan.**
- [ ] Pay all minimums on schedule (set reminders).
- [ ] Direct the extra amount to the target debt.
- [ ] Record the starting balances.

**Week 8: Review and refine.**
- [ ] Do the first monthly review (Appendix A).
- [ ] Compare actual to planned spending; change two lines.

### Month 3: Systems and future

**Week 9: Sinking funds.**
- [ ] List irregular annual costs; compute the monthly amount.
- [ ] Open or label the accounts.

**Week 10: Goals.**
- [ ] Complete a goal planner for two goals (Chapter 7).
- [ ] Check them against the budget.

**Week 11: Learn before investing.**
- [ ] Complete the risk self-check and the pre-investing checklist (Chapters 9 and 10).
- [ ] Find your country's official guidance on retirement accounts and employer contributions.
- [ ] Decide whether to seek regulated advice.

**Week 12: Protect and plan.**
- [ ] Complete the risk map; review insurance and beneficiaries.
- [ ] Write the net worth statement (Chapter 12).

**Week 13: Review and set the next 90 days.**
- [ ] Do the 90-day review: net worth change, debt balance change, emergency fund progress.
- [ ] Write three priorities for the next quarter.
- [ ] Book the annual review date in your calendar.

## What to do when the plan breaks

It will. A missed week, an overspent month or an emergency does not end the plan. Do the next smallest step: update the tracker, rebalance the budget lines, restart the transfer. The aim is a system that survives mistakes, not a record of perfect months.

# Appendix C. Glossary

**APR (annual percentage rate):** a standardised yearly cost of credit; definition set by local law.

**Asset:** something you own that has value.

**Asset allocation:** how money is divided among asset types.

**Avalanche method:** repaying the highest-rate debt first.

**Bond:** a loan to a government or company that pays interest.

**Budget:** a plan assigning income to uses.

**Cash flow:** money in minus money out over a period.

**Compound interest:** interest earned on earlier interest.

**Credit score or record:** a summary of repayment history used by lenders; systems differ by country.

**Diversification:** spreading money to reduce reliance on one outcome.

**Emergency fund:** cash held for unplanned essential costs.

**Excess or deductible:** the part of an insured loss you pay first.

**Fund:** a pooled investment holding many assets.

**Inflation:** a general rise in prices over time.

**Liability:** something you owe.

**Liquidity:** how quickly an asset can be turned into cash.

**Net worth:** assets minus liabilities.

**Premium:** the price of insurance.

**Principal:** the original amount borrowed or invested.

**Real return:** return after allowing for inflation.

**Rule of 72:** an approximation of doubling time.

**Secured debt:** debt backed by an asset the lender can claim.

**Sinking fund:** money set aside for a known future cost.

**Snowball method:** repaying the smallest balance first.

**Time horizon:** the time until you need the money.

**Volatility:** how much a value fluctuates.
