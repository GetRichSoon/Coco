# Chapter 6. Interview preparation

Preparation is mostly four jobs: know the employer, know your stories, sort the logistics, and practise aloud.

## Research the employer

Spend 45 minutes, not three days. Read the posting again, the employer's own website, recent news, and the profiles of your interviewers if you know them. Write down: what they sell or do, who they serve, one recent development, and one question it raises.

## Build your stories bank

Your answers will be drawn from 6 to 8 real stories covering work, study, volunteering or projects. Chapter 7 shows how; Appendix A has 30 prompts to find them. Map each story to the posting's top requirements.

## Logistics

- Confirm time zone, address or link, and interviewer names.
- Travel route and a spare 20 minutes, or test the link in advance.
- Bring copies of your resume, a notebook and your questions.
- Dress one level above the daily norm of that workplace if you can find out what it is.

## Remote and video interviews

- Test camera, microphone and internet the day before.
- Put the camera at eye height and light your face from the front.
- Close other applications and silence notifications.
- Look at the camera when you make key points.
- Have notes beside the screen, not on it. Keep a phone number for the interviewer in case of a connection failure.
- Pause slightly before answering; delay can make people talk over each other.

## Panel interviews

Write down names and roles as people introduce themselves. Answer mainly to the person who asked, but include eye contact with others. If one panellist is silent, still address the whole group at the end of each answer.

## Practice

Say your answers aloud, record yourself, and listen for filler, length and clarity. Practise with a friend who will interrupt with follow-ups, because real interviews contain them.

## Interview preparation checklist

- [ ] Posting marked into strong, partial and gap requirements
- [ ] Employer researched, one question prepared
- [ ] 6 to 8 stories written in short note form
- [ ] "Tell me about yourself" practised aloud
- [ ] Questions for the interviewer prepared (at least four)
- [ ] Logistics confirmed, route or link tested
- [ ] Documents and notes ready
- [ ] Sleep and meal planned

## Questions to ask the interviewer

```
What would success look like in the first six months?
What are the biggest challenges the team faces now?
How is feedback given and how often?
What are the next steps and timing?
```

Avoid asking things the website answers or opening with pay and leave.

# Chapter 7. Behavioural interviews

A behavioural question asks about something you did: "Tell me about a time when...". The reasoning is that past behaviour in similar situations gives evidence of how you work. The OPM page describes this as asking about past behaviour (opm.gov, accessed 2026-11-07).

## STAR

| Letter | Meaning | Share of answer |
|---|---|---|
| S | Situation: context in one or two sentences | 15% |
| T | Task: what you were responsible for | 10% |
| A | Action: what you personally did | 50% |
| R | Result: outcome and what you learned | 25% |

Those shares are a rule of thumb, not research. The key point is that Action is the core, and "I" must appear more than "we".

## Variants

- **CAR:** Challenge, Action, Result. Shorter, good for brief answers.
- **STAR+L:** add Learning at the end, useful for failure questions.
- **PREP (point, reason, example, point):** useful for opinion questions such as "Why this career?".
- **Past, present, future:** for "Tell me about yourself".

## Building a story bank

1. Use the 30 prompts in Appendix A. Write one or two lines per prompt. Many will be empty; that is normal.
2. Choose the six to eight richest. Include at least one failure, one conflict, one leadership moment, one improvement you made.
3. For each, write the true facts: dates, your role, numbers you actually know.
4. Tag each with the themes it can serve (teamwork, pressure, initiative).
5. Practise telling it in 90 seconds.

A single story can answer several questions. Reusing a good story is fine; reciting a memorised script is not. Know the facts, not the sentences.

## STAR worksheet

```
Story title:
Situation (where, when, who):
Task (my responsibility):
Action (3 specific things I did, in order):
Result (what changed; number if known):
What I learned:
Themes it can answer:
```

### Filled example (fictional)

```
Title: Late supplier at the bakery
Situation: Weekend rush, flour delivery missed, I was the
  duty supervisor.
Task: Keep the counter stocked for 200+ customers.
Action: Phoned two nearby shops for a loan, rebaked the best
  sellers first, told staff which items to prioritise.
Result: Sold out of only one line; owner changed supplier.
Learned: Keep a list of backup contacts.
Themes: pressure, initiative, problem-solving
```

## If you have no paid experience

Use study projects, volunteering, societies, family responsibilities, sports teams, part-time jobs. Interviewers know entry-level candidates draw from these.

# Chapter 8. Technical interviews

"Technical" means any question testing job-specific knowledge or skill. This book does not supply field facts; it gives a method you can use in any field.

## The preparation method

1. **Extract the skills.** From the posting, list the tools, methods and knowledge areas named.
2. **Rank them.** Mark those named most often and those you are weakest at.
3. **Find official or standard sources.** Use the course materials, professional body, tool documentation or a respected textbook for your field.
4. **Practise recall and application.** Explain each concept aloud in plain language, then solve two or three problems or cases without notes.
5. **Prepare one project.** Be able to explain one piece of real work end to end: goal, approach, mistakes, result.
6. **Practise thinking aloud.** Interviewers often assess process more than the final answer.

## Answering structure

Clarify the question, state assumptions, outline your approach, work through it, check the result, state limits. If you do not know, say what you do know, how you would find out, and where you would start.

## Examples of the same method in different fields (illustrative)

| Field | Typical task type | Your preparation |
|---|---|---|
| Software | Write or explain code | Practise problems; explain trade-offs aloud |
| Finance | Explain or build an analysis | Rebuild a simple model; explain assumptions |
| Marketing | Plan or critique a campaign | Prepare one campaign analysis with goals and measures |
| Operations | Improve a process | Map a process you know; show where delay occurs |

The exact content depends on the role. Do not rely on a list from a book for your field; use the employer's posting and your field's own resources.

## Case and take-home tasks

Ask about time limits and what is assessed. State assumptions in writing. Submit on time and a bit less ambitious rather than late.

## Common mistakes

Bluffing, going silent for a long time, skipping assumptions, memorising solutions without understanding, and failing to explain why.

# Chapter 9. Situational questions

A situational question describes a hypothetical: "What would you do if...". The OPM page lists hypothetical situations alongside past behaviour as structured interview methods (accessed 2026-11-07).

## Method

1. Restate the situation to confirm it.
2. Ask one or two clarifying questions, or state your assumptions.
3. Give your first step, then the reasoning.
4. Name the people involved and how you would communicate.
5. State what you would check afterwards.
6. Where possible, link to a real similar moment: "I faced something like this when...".

Situational answers should show judgement, ethics and willingness to ask for help. "It depends" is a valid start if you then say what it depends on.

## Pattern for a typical answer

```
First I would [confirm facts / safety]. Then I would
[action], because [reason]. I would involve [person] and
tell them [what]. Afterwards I would [follow-up]. If X
turned out to be true, I would instead [alternative].
```

Chapters 16, 17 and 20 contain many situational questions.

# Chapter 10. Communication skills

## Structure

Lead with the answer, then give one reason or story, then stop. If you can say it in 60 to 120 seconds, say it.

## Concision

Cut introductions ("That's a good question"), repeated qualifiers and detailed backstory. End with a clear closing sentence so the interviewer knows you are finished.

## Nerves

- Nerves are expected; interviewers often prefer a nervous but honest candidate over a polished evasive one.
- Slow your pace: breathe out slowly before you speak.
- Prepare the first 30 seconds of "Tell me about yourself" so you start steadily.
- Drink water; a pause to sip is acceptable.

## Listening

Note keywords in the question. If it has two parts, answer both. Watch for follow-up cues: a raised eyebrow or a pause often means the interviewer wants more detail.

## When your mind goes blank

Say: "Let me take a moment to think." Pause for five seconds. If nothing comes, offer a related story, or say "Could we come back to this question?" and return at the end.

## Asking for clarification

```
Could you tell me more about what you mean by [term]?
Do you mean in my last role, or in general?
Would you like a recent example or the most challenging one?
```

Clarifying shows care. Do it once or twice per interview, not every answer.

## Body language and voice

Sit upright, keep hands visible and relaxed, speak at a steady pace. Smile naturally when appropriate. On video, nod briefly to show you are listening.

# Chapter 11. Salary negotiation

Negotiation is a conversation about a fair match between your value and the employer's budget. No script guarantees a better offer; employers may have fixed bands.

## Research

- Official statistics: in the US the Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook lists pay for many occupations and its website links to detailed wage data by occupation (bls.gov/ooh, accessed 2026-11-07). Other countries have their own statistical agencies.
- Posted ranges, where employers publish them.
- Professional bodies and unions.
- Conversations with people in similar roles (ask about ranges, not exact salaries).
- Salary sites: treat as rough, since data quality varies.

## Build your range

```
Floor: lowest number I would accept
Target: realistic number from research
Stretch: upper end if I can justify it
Non-pay items: leave, hours, remote days, training,
  start date, review date, bonus, relocation
```

## Timing

Avoid discussing pay until you understand the role, ideally until the employer is ready to offer. If asked early, give a researched range and say you are flexible depending on the whole package. Negotiate after the written or verbal offer, not before. Thank them first.

## Country differences

Practices differ: in some countries employers publish pay ranges, in others asking about previous salary is restricted or normal, and in some regions negotiating is expected while in others the offer is fixed by a grade scale. Rules differ by country and sometimes by region. Check your government's labour website or a local professional body before you rely on a rule.

## Scripts

### 1. Responding to an early salary question

```
I would like to learn more about the role first. Based on my
research for similar roles in [city], I am looking at
[range], and I am open to discussing the full package.
```

### 2. Asking for more after an offer

```
Thank you, I am excited about the role. Based on [research
and my experience in X], I was hoping for [figure]. Is there
flexibility?
```

### 3. Offer is fixed

```
I understand the salary band is fixed. Could we look at
[start date / training budget / review at six months]?
```

### 4. Comparing with another offer (only if true)

```
I have another offer at [range], but your role is my first
choice. Is there anything you can do on salary?
```

### 5. Asking for time

```
Thank you for the offer. May I have until [date] to review
it and respond?
```

### 6. Accepting

```
I am pleased to accept at [salary] with the terms we
discussed. Please send the written offer.
```

### 7. Declining politely

```
Thank you for the offer and your time. I have decided to
decline because [brief reason]. I hope we can stay in touch.
```

If they say no, decide beforehand whether you will accept anyway. Never state a competing offer that does not exist.

# Chapter 12. Interview follow-ups

Send a thank-you within 24 hours. It should be short, specific and accurate.

## Thank-you email templates

### 1. After a first interview

```
Subject: Thank you, [Role] interview

Dear [Name],
Thank you for speaking with me today about the [role].
I especially enjoyed hearing about [specific topic]. It
reinforced my interest, and I believe my experience in
[relevant skill] could help with [need discussed].
Please let me know if I can provide anything further.
Kind regards,
[Name]
```

### 2. After a panel

```
Dear [Names],
Thank you for the time you spent with me. [Name 1], your
point on [topic] was useful, and [Name 2], thank you for
explaining [topic]. I would be glad to contribute to...
```

### 3. After a final interview, adding a clarification

```
Dear [Name],
Thank you for today. One point I would like to add: in
answer to your question about [topic], [short addition].
I look forward to the next step.
```

### 4. When you want to keep the door open after a rejection

```
Dear [Name],
Thank you for letting me know. I appreciated the chance to
meet the team. If other roles arise, I would welcome the
chance to apply. Would you mind sharing any feedback?
```

## Follow-up scripts

1. **No reply after the stated date:** "I am following up on the [role]. I am still very interested and can provide any further information."
2. **Waiting after a week with no timeline:** "Could you let me know if the timeline for the decision has changed?"
3. **After a rejection, asking for feedback:** "I would appreciate any feedback that could help me improve."
4. **After accepting another offer, withdrawing:** "Thank you for your time. I have accepted another position and would like to withdraw my application."
5. **Contacting a referee in advance:** "I have given your name as a reference for [role]. Here is the posting and my resume."

Follow up at most twice, with a week between, unless told otherwise.

# Chapter 13. Job search planning

A search is easier when treated as a weekly routine. Track every application so you can follow up and learn.

## Application tracker

| Company | Role | Date | Stage | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example Ltd | Analyst | 3 Mar | Interview 1 | Thank-you sent; follow up 12 Mar |

Add columns in a spreadsheet for: link, contact name, resume version, source, salary range, notes. Use a filter by stage, a date format column and conditional formatting for overdue follow-ups. Keep one row per application and update it the same day.

## 30 day job search planner

**Week 1: Foundations**
- Define target roles (two or three) and locations.
- Write the master resume and story bank (Appendix A).
- Update LinkedIn using Chapter 4.
- Set up the tracker.

**Week 2: Applications**
- Tailor and submit 5 to 8 applications to roles you fit.
- Write a base cover letter and adapt it.
- Message 3 people for short conversations.
- Practise "Tell me about yourself".

**Week 3: Practice**
- Practise 20 questions from Chapters 14 to 20 aloud.
- Do one mock interview with a friend or career service.
- Continue applications; follow up on week 2.
- Research salary range for targets.

**Week 4: Review**
- Check the tracker: what stage are applications at?
- Adjust resume based on responses.
- Prepare for any interviews with the checklist in Chapter 6.
- Plan the next 30 days.

Numbers here are suggestions, not targets proven to work; scale to your time. Rest and sleep belong in the plan.

## Networking

Ask for information, not jobs. Example message: "I am a final year student interested in [field]. Could I ask you two questions about your path?" Thank everyone you speak with.
