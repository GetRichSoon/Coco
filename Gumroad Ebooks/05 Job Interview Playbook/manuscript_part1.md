# Introduction

This book has one purpose: to help you walk into an interview able to answer the questions you are most likely to be asked, truthfully and clearly. It also covers what happens before the interview (resume, applicant tracking systems, LinkedIn, cover letters) and after it (follow-ups, salary negotiation, planning your search).

It is written for university students, recent graduates and entry-level applicants, for experienced professionals moving up, and for career changers. Every one of the 100 questions in Chapters 14 to 20 has a separate **Beginner adaptation** and **Experienced candidate adaptation**, so you can use the same question whether you have six months of experience or sixteen years.

## The one rule: your answers must be true

Every example answer in this book is fictional and written to show structure. Do not copy them. Do not memorise them. An interviewer who asks a follow-up will find the gap between a borrowed story and your real memory within a minute, and an invented story is dishonest and can end an offer or a job later. Use each example to see the shape of a good answer, then build yours from things that really happened to you. Chapter 7 and the story bank in Appendix A show how to find those stories, including when you have little paid experience.

## What this book does not do

- It does not promise a job, an interview or a salary. Hiring depends on many things you do not control.
- It does not claim that any resume layout beats any applicant tracking system. Chapter 3 explains what is publicly known.
- It gives no field-specific facts about industries. Chapter 8 gives a method for preparing technical questions in any field.
- Country rules on pay disclosure, résumé conventions and employment law differ. Check local official sources.

## How to use it

1. Read Chapters 1 to 13 once, in order, to build your documents and your method.
2. Use Chapters 14 to 20 as a question bank. Read the question, cover the example, write your own answer from a real story, then compare.
3. Use the templates, checklists and the 30 day planner in the appendices.

# Chapter 1. Understanding the hiring process

A hiring process is a series of filters, each answering a different question for the employer. Knowing the stage you are in tells you what to prepare.

## The usual stages

| Stage | Employer's question | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Job posting | Who do we need? | Read it as a checklist |
| Application | Does this person match? | Tailored resume, short letter |
| Screening | Are they real, reachable, interested? | Short call or form |
| Interviews | Can they do it and fit here? | Prepared stories |
| Assessment | Can they show it? | Test, task, presentation |
| References and offer | Is it safe to hire? | Briefed referees, negotiation |

Not every employer uses every stage. Small firms may do one conversation; large ones may use five steps over weeks.

## Who is on the other side

A recruiter screens for basic fit and logistics. A hiring manager decides who can do the work. Team members judge whether they can work with you. Human resources handles process and the offer. Each reads your answers differently: the recruiter listens for a match to the posting, the manager for results, peers for how you treat people.

## Structured interviews

Many employers ask every candidate the same questions and score answers against the same standard. The US Office of Personnel Management describes structured interviews as measuring job related competencies by asking about past behaviour, about behaviour in hypothetical situations, or both, with the same questions and rating scale for all candidates (opm.gov, accessed 2026-11-07). This is why the book separates behavioural questions (Chapter 7) from situational ones (Chapter 9).

## Reading a job posting

Copy the posting into a document. Mark three groups: requirements you meet clearly, requirements you partly meet, and gaps. Underline repeated words; repetition shows priority. Your resume, letter and stories should cover the first two groups, and you should have an honest line for the third (see Q96 on gaps).

## What interviewers decide

Interviewers generally weigh three things: can you do the job, will you do it, and can they work with you. Every answer you give should support at least one.

# Chapter 2. Resume writing

A resume is a short, scannable document that proves you can do the work described in one particular posting.

## Principles

- One to two pages. One page is normal for students and early career; two for long careers.
- Reverse chronological order for most people, because it is the easiest for both people and software to read.
- Each bullet: action verb, what you did, result or scale. Use real numbers only if you know them; otherwise describe the outcome plainly.
- Tailor the top third (summary, skills, first bullets) to each posting by using its words where they honestly describe you.
- Do not lie, inflate titles or hide gaps with tricks. Ohio State Engineering Career Services warns against false information and hidden keywords (ecs.osu.edu, accessed 2026-11-07).

## Bullet formula

```
Action verb + task + method or tool + result/scale
Organised weekly stock counts using a shared spreadsheet,
cutting missing-item reports in my store section.
```

If you do not know the number, do not invent one. "Reduced" needs something real behind it.

## Plain text ATS safe structure

```
FULL NAME
City, Country | phone | email | LinkedIn URL

SUMMARY (2 lines, optional for students)
SKILLS (grouped, comma separated)
EXPERIENCE
  Job title, Employer, City, Month Year - Month Year
  - bullet
  - bullet
EDUCATION
  Degree, Institution, Year
PROJECTS / CERTIFICATIONS (if relevant)
```

## Career changers

Lead with a skills section mapped to the target role, then experience. Rename nothing falsely; instead add a line such as "Transferable: stakeholder reporting, budget tracking" and put relevant projects or courses before unrelated jobs only if the layout remains chronological and clear.

## Resume checklist

- [ ] Contact details in the body, not only in a header
- [ ] Standard headings (Experience, Education, Skills)
- [ ] Dates consistent
- [ ] No tables, text boxes, photos or graphics
- [ ] Each bullet shows an action and an outcome
- [ ] Proofread aloud; ask someone else to read it

# Chapter 3. Applicant tracking systems

An applicant tracking system (ATS) is software employers use to collect applications, store candidate data and manage stages. It is a database and workflow tool, not a magic gate.

## What is publicly said

I found no vendor neutral test showing how each major system parses each layout, so this chapter states only what employer and university sources say.

- Roche's careers FAQ says parsing extracts and organises resume data to help fill application fields and suggest matches; recruiters review each applicant and the attached CV; candidates should check that the data landed in the right fields (accessed 2026-11-07).
- The same FAQ advises against tables, text boxes, images, columns, headers and footers, and unusual section headings; it recommends standard fonts, contact details at the top and reverse chronological order, and says .docx parses most accurately.
- Ohio State Engineering Career Services says some systems parse the text of uploaded resumes, which can change formatting, and recommends PDF so recruiters see the intended layout; it also says recruiters, not the software, do most filtering, and that strong content matters more than gimmicks (accessed 2026-11-07).

These two sources disagree on file type. The safe rule is simple: follow the file type the posting asks for. If none is stated, ask or use the one the application form accepts.

## Practical consequences

1. Use a simple one column layout and standard headings.
2. Use the posting's wording where it truthfully describes you (job title, tools, qualifications).
3. After uploading, check the auto filled fields and fix errors.
4. Keep a plain version for applications and, if you want, a designed version to send to people directly.
5. Do not hide keywords in white text. Humans see it eventually and it reads as dishonest.

## What no one can promise

No layout, keyword count or score tool guarantees that a resume passes. How a given employer configures filtering varies and is not visible to you. Spend your effort on a truthful, relevant resume and on applying to roles you actually fit.

# Chapter 4. LinkedIn optimisation

This chapter states only what LinkedIn's official help page "Create a good LinkedIn profile" says (accessed 2026-11-07). Anything else here is general advice and is labelled as such.

## What LinkedIn says

- **Photo:** a professional photo adds credibility; members with a photo get up to 2X more profile views. You can add a background image.
- **Headline:** it can be rewritten to highlight an area of expertise or show personality.
- **About:** describe your mission, motivation and skills in one or two paragraphs; bullet points are acceptable.
- **Work and education:** keep them current and aligned with your goals; add media samples to show your work.
- **Skills:** list relevant skills; connections can endorse them and you can take assessments.
- **Recommendations:** request them from connections; there is no limit and you can hide ones that do not fit.
- **Profile strength meter:** a gauge of your profile's strength.

## General advice (not from LinkedIn)

Make the profile agree with your resume on titles and dates. Use the headline for what you do and want, not only your current title. Write the About in first person with one concrete example of your work.

## LinkedIn profile checklist

- [ ] Clear recent photo
- [ ] Headline states role and focus
- [ ] About in one or two paragraphs
- [ ] Experience and education current and consistent with resume
- [ ] Relevant skills listed
- [ ] At least one recommendation requested
- [ ] Contact details visible as you prefer
- [ ] Profile strength reviewed

# Chapter 5. Cover letters

A cover letter answers two questions the resume cannot: why this role, and why you. Keep it under one page, around 200 to 300 words.

## Structure

1. Opening: the role and one sentence on why it fits you.
2. Evidence: one or two real achievements matched to the posting.
3. Interest: something specific about the employer you actually learned.
4. Close: availability and thanks.

## Template

```
Dear [Name / Hiring Team],

I am applying for the [role] position. [One sentence connecting
your most relevant strength to their main need.]

In [role/project], I [action and result]. This matches your
need for [requirement from the posting].

I am drawn to [specific true detail about the organisation]
because [your reason].

I would welcome a conversation. I am available from [date].

Yours sincerely,
[Name]
```

## Example (fictional)

```
Dear Ms Ito,

I am applying for the Junior Content Editor role. I edit and
publish a student newsletter read by about 400 people, which is
close to the weekly cycle you describe.

Last term I introduced a simple checklist for fact-checking and
corrections dropped to nearly none, as the faculty adviser noted.

I admire your team's practice of publishing corrections openly,
and I would like to work in that culture.

I am available from 1 June.

Yours sincerely,
Kenji Mori
```

## Common mistakes

Repeating the resume line by line, using "To whom it may concern" when a name is findable, writing about what you want rather than what you offer, and sending the same letter to every employer.
