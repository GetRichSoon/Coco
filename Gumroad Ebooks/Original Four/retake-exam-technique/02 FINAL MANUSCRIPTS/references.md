## A note on sources

The learning techniques in this book draw on general research on the testing effect, spaced repetition, test anxiety and answer changing, and on ordinary multiple-choice exam practice. They are described in plain terms and without figures, because no figure would apply to your exam, and none is needed to use the method. The studies below were not carried out on your exam or on professional licensing candidates in particular, and none of them is a promise of a result.

## Sources

Each source was opened on 2026-11-07. Where only the publisher's or repository's abstract page was read, that is said.

- Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). "Test-enhanced learning." *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249 to 255. https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x/ (abstract page, accessed 2026-11-07). Supports the testing effect: restudying gave better recall five minutes later, but practice tests gave better recall after two days and one week.
- Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). "Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis." *Psychological Bulletin*, 132(3), 354 to 380. https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1771/ (abstract page, accessed 2026-11-07). Supports spaced review: the best gap between reviews grew as the time the material had to be remembered grew.
- Minihan, S., Samimi, Z., & Schweizer, S. (2021). "The effectiveness of affective compared to neutral working memory training in university students with test anxiety." *Behaviour Research and Therapy*, 147, 103974. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7611905 (accessed 2026-11-07). Used only for how test anxiety is commonly described: worry (concerns and ruminations about performance) and emotionality (bodily responses).
- Kruger, J., Wirtz, D., & Miller, D. T. (2005). "Counterfactual thinking and the first instinct fallacy." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 88(5), 725 to 735. https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/counterfactual-thinking-first-instinct-fallacy (publisher summary page, accessed 2026-11-07). Supports the statement that answer changes more often go from wrong to right than the reverse, and that the belief in sticking with a first answer persists because the opposite changes are more memorable.
