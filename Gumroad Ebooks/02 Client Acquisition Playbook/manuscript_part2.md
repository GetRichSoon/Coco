# Chapter 6. Finding leads

## What a lead is

A lead is a named person at a named business who fits your ideal customer profile and whom you can contact by an honest route. A company name alone is not a lead. A list of 500 unverified emails is not leads either.

## Where to look, in order of quality

1. **Your own network:** past clients, former colleagues, people who have praised your work. Write 30 names this week.
2. **Referrals and introductions:** people connected to your past clients and peers who serve the same buyers without competing.
3. **Communities where your buyers ask questions:** professional forums, local business groups, industry newsletters with open comment sections. Observe the rules of each space.
4. **Public directories and associations:** member lists published for the purpose of being found.
5. **Trigger sources:** job ads, new product launches, funding or hiring news, new website pages, public reviews.
6. **Search and maps:** for local niches, search by service and area, then verify.

## How to build a list of 50

Use one row per lead. Required fields: name, role, company, website, source, trigger, contact route, date found. If you cannot fill trigger and source, do not add the lead yet.

1. Define the filter from your ICP (size, region, type).
2. Collect 80 names from two sources.
3. Remove duplicates, competitors and anyone matching a disqualifier.
4. Find the right person: owner for small firms; head of the relevant function for larger ones.
5. Write one line of relevant observation per lead (the trigger). If you cannot write it in under two minutes, drop the lead.

Do not buy scraped lists, and do not use tools that harvest LinkedIn data. Appendix A explains why. Build smaller lists by hand; 50 well researched leads outperform 500 careless ones in the only way that matters here, they are safe to contact and easy to personalise.

## Warm outreach templates

Warm messages work because the relationship does the heavy lifting. Keep them short, specific and easy to decline.

### O1. Referral ask to a happy client

**When to use:** within a month of a successful delivery, or at a natural review point.

**Instructions:** Name the result they got. Describe the kind of person you want to meet in one sentence. Offer to write the introduction text for them.

```
Subject: A small favour, if you are happy to

Hi Priya,

I am glad the new invoicing routine has cut your month end
to a single afternoon. I am looking for two or three more
architecture studios with 5 to 15 staff who have the same
late invoice problem.

Do you know a studio director who might find a short chat
useful? If so, I can write a two line note you can forward,
so it takes you under a minute. No pressure if no one comes
to mind.

Thanks again,
Tom
```

### O2. Warm introduction request to a mutual contact

**When to use:** you spotted a prospect who is connected to someone you know.

**Instructions:** Say why this person, what you would offer them, and make it easy for your contact to say no.

```
Subject: Could you introduce me to Ellis?

Hi Marcus,

I saw you are connected to Ellis Wong at Ridgeline Studio.
I help small studios fix late invoicing and have worked with
two similar firms. Would you be comfortable introducing us?

If it helps, here is a note you can forward:
"Ellis, meet Tom. He helped our studio with billing and I
thought a short chat could be useful. Up to you."

If you would rather not, I completely understand.

Tom
```

### O3. Forwardable introduction blurb

**When to use:** after someone agrees to introduce you.

**Instructions:** Write the message for the introducer to send, in their voice, with no hype and a clear next step.

```
Hi Ellis, I wanted to introduce Tom Reed. He set up our
stage billing last year and our invoices now go out weekly.
You mentioned slow cash flow last month, so I thought a
short chat might help. I have copied him here; reply if
you want to connect, ignore if not.
```

### O4. Past client reactivation

**When to use:** a client who finished more than 3 months ago and left on good terms.

**Instructions:** Do not start with a sales pitch. Refer to something specific from the project, give a reason to write now, and offer a low pressure option.

```
Subject: Checking in on the billing setup

Hi Sam,

It has been about eight months since we set up your project
billing. I was wondering how the monthly report is holding
up as you added two new staff.

If anything has drifted, I can do a 45 minute review and send
you a list of fixes, nothing more. If everything is fine,
I would still like to hear what is working.

Tom
```

### O5. Community reply that offers help

**When to use:** someone in a public community asks a question you can answer.

**Instructions:** Answer fully in public first. Mention your service only if the rules allow it, and never message someone privately unless they asked or the rules clearly permit it.

```
Reply on a public thread:

"For project based billing, the most common cause of late
invoices is that the invoice trigger is a date rather than a
stage. Try: 1) list your stages, 2) assign each a percentage,
3) set the invoice to be created when the stage is signed
off. If it helps, I can share the template I use in this
thread. (I do this for studios professionally, so feel free
to ask follow ups here.)"
```

### O6. Event or meeting follow up

**When to use:** within 48 hours of meeting someone at an event, webinar or call.

**Instructions:** Mention one real detail from the conversation. Offer one useful thing, not a pitch.

```
Subject: Good to meet you at Thursday's studio meetup

Hi Neha,

You mentioned that invoices wait until the whole project is
done. That is fixable without new software. I have a one page
guide to stage billing; shall I send it over?

Either way, good to meet you.

Tom
```

## Common mistakes and fixes

**Mistake:** Hoarding names without an observation.
**Fix:** No trigger, no lead.

**Mistake:** Asking for referrals only when work is thin.
**Fix:** Ask after every successful delivery.

**Mistake:** Using a tool that scrapes profiles.
**Fix:** Research by hand; read Appendix A.

# Chapter 7. Cold email prospecting

## When cold email is appropriate

Cold email is a first message to someone who has not asked to hear from you. Done well, it is a short, relevant note to a person with a likely problem. Done badly, it is spam. Before sending, read Appendix A. In short: be honest about who you are, do not mislead in the subject line, include a way to opt out, honour opt outs, and know the rules in the country of your recipient. Rules differ and this is not legal advice.

## Anatomy of a message

A good cold email has five parts, in 60 to 120 words:

1. **Subject:** plain, specific, matches the content.
2. **Opening line:** about them, based on something true and observable.
3. **Problem or idea:** one sentence on what you noticed or can help with.
4. **Proof:** one line of evidence.
5. **Small ask:** a yes or no question, not a 45 minute call.

A small ask lowers the effort needed to reply. Forum discussions among freelancers repeat the same observation: a request for a long call from a stranger tends to get ignored, while a short question gets answered. That is anecdotal, so test it with your own list.

## Sending practicalities

- Send from a real address on your own domain. Use your real name and a signature with your business name and postal address or the identification required where you operate.
- Send in small batches, 10 to 20 a day, and personalise each one.
- Keep a record of everyone who asks you to stop, and never contact them again.
- Do not hide behind a no reply address, fake "Re:" subject lines or false familiarity.

## Eight cold email templates

### O7. Trigger based opener

**When to use:** you found a real, recent event (hire, launch, funding, new service page).

**Instructions:** State the trigger in the first line. Connect it to one problem you solve. Ask a yes or no question.

```
Subject: Your new Bath office

Hi Ellis,

I saw Ridgeline opened a second office in Bath last month.
Studios that add a location often find project billing gets
harder to track across sites.

I help small studios set up stage billing so the director
sees profit per project in one report. Is that something you
are looking at this quarter?

Tom Reed, Reed Books, [postal address]
Reply "stop" and I will not write again.
```

### O8. Observation about a visible problem

**When to use:** you can see a specific, fixable issue (a broken form, slow page, unclear pricing page).

**Instructions:** Describe what you saw, say how you saw it, and offer to explain. Never exaggerate the damage.

```
Subject: Contact form on your services page

Hi Claire,

When I tried your contact form on the services page this
morning, it showed an error after I pressed send (Chrome on
desktop). You may already know.

I fix small website issues like this for studios, and can send
you the steps I took to reproduce it. Want me to send them?

Jo Martin, Martin Web Support
```

### O9. Mini audit offer

**When to use:** you can give a small piece of value in under 30 minutes.

**Instructions:** Offer a specific, bounded audit. Say what they get and that there is no obligation. Only offer what you will deliver.

```
Subject: A 10 point check of your booking page

Hi Dev,

I help physiotherapy clinics get more booking requests from
their existing website traffic. I would be glad to review
your booking page and send a 10 point list of fixes, free,
no obligation.

Should I send it to this address?

Aisha Khan, Clinic Growth Notes
```

### O10. Short case note

**When to use:** you have a relevant, true result to share.

**Instructions:** Describe a similar client in one sentence, the result in one sentence, then the question. Label fictional examples as examples in your own materials.

```
Subject: Quote follow up at small builders

Hi Greg,

A 6 person building firm I worked with answered quotes in
five days on average. After we set up a quote template and a
two day reply rule, the average fell to two days (their own
tracking, over one quarter).

Do your quotes ever wait longer than you would like?

Lena Ortiz, Ortiz Operations
```

### O11. Permission question

**When to use:** you are unsure the person owns the problem.

**Instructions:** Ask who is the right person, in one line. This lowers risk for the recipient.

```
Subject: Who handles event bookings at Harbour Hotel?

Hi Rosa,

I help hotels tidy up how corporate event enquiries are
handled. Are you the right person to ask about that, or
should I write to someone else?

Thanks for your time,
Mei Chen, Event Desk Support
```

### O12. Partner or agency to agency note

**When to use:** you want to subcontract or refer work with a complementary firm.

**Instructions:** Say what you do that they do not, how the arrangement could work, and how you protect their client relationship.

```
Subject: Overflow design help for your web projects

Hi Karim,

Your portfolio is strong on web builds. I am a UX designer who
works as a white label partner for small agencies on a project
basis, and I do not contact your clients directly.

Would it be useful to have someone on call for overflow work?
I can send two sample files.

Jade Lowe, Lowe UX
```

### O13. Genuine mention of a shared connection

**When to use:** you really do share a contact, and they have said it is fine to mention them.

**Instructions:** Get permission before using the name. Never imply an endorsement that was not given.

```
Subject: Marcus Bell suggested I write

Hi Ellis,

Marcus Bell suggested I get in touch (he said I could use his
name). I helped his studio fix late invoices, and he thought
you might be dealing with something similar.

Open to a short note on how it worked?

Tom Reed
```

### O14. Hiring signal opener

**When to use:** the company posted a job that you could cover as a service or reduce the need for.

**Instructions:** Mention the ad, suggest an alternative or a bridge, and make the ask low risk. Do not mock the job.

```
Subject: Your open bookkeeper role

Hi Hana,

I saw Northgate Studio is hiring a part time bookkeeper. Until
you find the right person, I could cover month end on a fixed
monthly fee so books do not fall behind.

Would a short bridge arrangement help?

Tom Reed
```

## Handling replies

- **Yes:** reply within the hour with two time options.
- **Not now:** thank them, ask when to follow up, and log the date.
- **Wrong person:** ask for the right contact.
- **Stop:** confirm that you will not write again, add them to your do not contact list, and do so.

## Common mistakes and fixes

**Mistake:** Writing about yourself for four paragraphs.
**Fix:** One line about you, the rest about them.

**Mistake:** Template language such as "I hope this finds you well".
**Fix:** Replace with a first line that could only be sent to this person.

**Mistake:** Judging a template after five sends.
**Fix:** Judge after at least 30 sends of the same version.

# Chapter 8. LinkedIn outreach

## What LinkedIn is good for

LinkedIn lets you see roles, shared connections and public activity, so it suits relationship led outreach to named buyers. It is also a place with strict rules. LinkedIn's help pages state that it does not allow third party software or browser extensions that scrape, change the appearance of, or automate activity on the site, and that using such tools breaches the User Agreement and can lead to account restriction. This book therefore recommends manual outreach only. Send messages yourself, one at a time, in your own words.

## A sensible manual routine

1. Optimise your headline: buyer, problem, result.
2. Spend 10 minutes a day on activity: comment usefully on three posts from your target buyers.
3. Send 5 to 10 personalised connection requests a day, no more than you can write properly.
4. When someone accepts, thank them and wait. Do not pitch in the first message after accept.
5. Move interested people to email or a call.

## Six LinkedIn templates

### O15. Connection note

**When to use:** first contact with a relevant person.

**Instructions:** Keep it under 200 characters, mention a real connection point, and make no pitch. Check the current character limit on your account.

```
Hi Ellis, I enjoyed your post on running a studio across two
sites. I work with small studios on billing. Happy to connect.
```

### O16. Follow up after connection accepted

**When to use:** 1 to 2 days after acceptance.

**Instructions:** Thank them and ask a question related to their work. Do not attach a pitch.

```
Thanks for connecting, Ellis. Quick question out of curiosity:
with the Bath office open, how are you tracking profit per
project? No agenda, I am collecting how studios handle this.
```

### O17. Comment to message bridge

**When to use:** a person engaged with your comment or you with theirs.

**Instructions:** Refer to the exact comment. Offer something relevant and wait for their interest.

```
Hi Neha, your comment on stage billing made me think of a one
page checklist I use with studios. Would you like me to send
it? Takes a minute to read.
```

### O18. Post event note

**When to use:** after a webinar or online event where you both took part.

**Instructions:** Refer to something they said; do not mass message attendees.

```
Hi Daniel, your question about client deposits in today's
webinar was a good one. I wrote a short note on how some
studios handle deposits. Want me to share it?
```

### O19. Reply to a buyer signal post

**When to use:** the prospect posted that they are looking for help with your type of work.

**Instructions:** Reply publicly first, briefly. Send a private message only if invited or if it adds value.

```
Hi Anika, saw your post asking for help with onboarding
emails. I do exactly this for software start ups. Happy to
share two sample sequences if useful. Should I send them here?
```

### O20. Move to a call, politely

**When to use:** after at least two friendly exchanges and a clear need.

**Instructions:** Offer the call as an option and state the time cost.

```
Ellis, it sounds like profit by project is the open question.
I can show you a one page report layout in 20 minutes if that
would help. If it is easier, I can send it in writing instead.
```

## What not to do on LinkedIn

- Do not use automation tools, scraping tools or bots, even those that claim to be safe.
- Do not send identical messages to hundreds of people.
- Do not pitch in connection requests or immediately after acceptance.
- Do not create fake profiles or fake engagement.
- Do not add people to a mailing list without asking.

## Common mistakes and fixes

**Mistake:** Long pitches in the first message.
**Fix:** Shorten; make the first two messages about them.

**Mistake:** Sending 100 requests in a day.
**Fix:** Send fewer and write properly; volume limits are also set by LinkedIn.

**Mistake:** Neglecting your profile.
**Fix:** Each message sends people to your profile; make it state your offer.

# Chapter 9. Content driven lead generation

## What it can and cannot do

Content lets buyers find and judge you before talking to you. It is slower than outreach and compounds over time. Treat it as a second engine started alongside outreach, not a replacement for it.

## Three kinds of content that attract buyers

1. **Problem content:** names a problem in the buyer's words and explains causes.
2. **Proof content:** shows how you work and what changed for a client (with consent).
3. **Process content:** teaches a method buyers could try themselves; it shows competence and builds trust.

## A weekly rhythm that is possible alongside client work

| Day | Task | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Mon | Note one question from a client or prospect | 10 min |
| Tue | Write a 150 to 250 word post answering it | 30 min |
| Wed | Publish and comment on 3 buyer posts | 20 min |
| Thu | Reply to all comments | 10 min |
| Fri | Update one reusable piece (guide, checklist) | 30 min |

### A reusable post structure

```
Line 1: A specific problem a buyer recognises.
Lines 2 to 5: Why it happens (two or three causes).
Lines 6 to 9: A method they can apply (steps).
Last line: An invitation to ask questions.
```

### Filled example (fictional)

```
Why do invoices go out late at small studios?

Usually not laziness. Three causes I see often:
1. Billing is tied to project end, not stages.
2. Nobody owns invoice day.
3. Time data lives in three places.

Try this: list your stages, give each a share of the fee,
and pick one weekday as invoice day.

Questions about stage billing? Ask below.
```

## Lead magnets with consent

A lead magnet is a useful document in exchange for an email address. If you collect addresses, say plainly what you will send and how to unsubscribe, store them safely, and send only what you promised. Under EU data protection law, personal data must be processed lawfully, fairly and transparently, for specified purposes, and kept no longer than necessary (European Commission, accessed 2026-11-07).

## Turning content attention into conversations

- Reply to every comment within a day.
- When someone shows a clear need in a comment or message, offer a call; do not push.
- Keep a list of people who engage often. They are warm leads for Chapter 6 outreach.

## Common mistakes and fixes

**Mistake:** Posting about trends rather than buyer problems.
**Fix:** Every post answers one real question from a buyer.

**Mistake:** Posting for two weeks, then stopping.
**Fix:** Use a smaller rhythm (one post a week) that you can keep for six months.

**Mistake:** No call to action.
**Fix:** End with a clear, low pressure next step.
