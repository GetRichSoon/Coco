# Chapter 10. Discovery calls

## Purpose

A discovery call is a conversation to decide whether there is a real problem, a real budget and a fit. It is not a pitch. You are collecting the information needed to qualify (Chapter 11) and to write a proposal (Chapter 12). The person who asks the best questions usually earns the right to propose.

## Structure of a 30 minute call

| Minutes | Part | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 3 | Open | State purpose, time, and how it will end |
| 3 to 15 | Situation and problem | Understand what is happening and why now |
| 15 to 22 | Impact and past attempts | Cost of the problem; what has failed |
| 22 to 27 | Decision and budget | Who decides, when, what range |
| 27 to 30 | Next step | Agree a dated action |

### Opening line

```
Thanks for making time. I have 30 minutes blocked. I would like
to understand what is going on, tell you honestly whether I can
help, and agree a next step either way. Is that okay?
```

### Rules that make calls useful

- Talk less than 40% of the time.
- Write the buyer's own words down; use them in the proposal.
- Ask "what else?" before moving on.
- Ask about budget plainly and early enough to avoid wasting both sides' time.
- End with a dated next step, such as "I will send the proposal on Thursday and we speak Monday at 10."

## Ten scripts by situation

Each script is a question set. The example shows the questions filled in for a fictional business, Reed Books, a bookkeeping practice for small studios. Adapt the wording to your trade.

### C1. Inbound lead (they contacted you)

**When to use:** a form submission or email asking for help.

**Instructions:** Confirm what prompted the enquiry, then follow the structure. Do not assume their stated request is the real problem.

```
1. What made you reach out this week?
2. What happens today when an invoice needs to go out?
3. What is the effect when invoices are late?
4. What have you tried, and what happened?
5. If this were solved by month end, what changes for you?
6. Who else is involved in a decision like this?
7. Do you have a budget range in mind?
8. When would you like this running?
Close: "I will send a written proposal by Thursday."
```

### C2. Referral call

**When to use:** someone you know introduced you.

**Instructions:** Thank the referrer, check what they told the prospect, and do not rely on borrowed trust.

```
1. Marcus mentioned you are opening a second office. How is
   that going?
2. What did he tell you about what I did for him?
3. What is different about your situation?
4. Where do you feel the most friction today?
5. What would a good outcome look like in three months?
6. Who else will weigh in?
7. What range have you considered?
Close: "Shall I send a short proposal and a reference you can
call?" (only if the reference has agreed)
```

### C3. Cold reply call

**When to use:** someone replied to cold outreach and agreed to talk.

**Instructions:** They said yes to a short chat, not to a pitch. Re-state why you wrote, then ask more than you tell.

```
1. You replied to my note about project billing. What stood out?
2. How are you tracking profit by project right now?
3. How long does month end take?
4. Is this a priority this quarter or a someday item?
5. What would make it urgent?
6. Who would sign off on a fix?
7. Is it worth a more detailed look?
Close: "Let me send three questions in writing so I can come
back with a concrete suggestion."
```

### C4. Price first buyer

**When to use:** the first question is "how much do you charge?"

**Instructions:** Give a range honestly if you have one, then ask what they are comparing against. Price without context invites a race to the bottom, but hiding price wastes time.

```
1. Happy to talk price. Typical projects like this range from
   [your range], depending on scope. Does that sit in the
   right area?
2. What are you comparing this with?
3. What does the cheaper option not cover that you need?
4. What is the cost of the problem staying as is?
5. What matters most: cost, speed, or low risk?
6. What budget did you set aside?
7. Shall I scope the lowest sensible option first?
Close: "I will send two options with different scope."
```

### C5. Existing client expansion

**When to use:** a current client may need additional work.

**Instructions:** Review results first. Ask what changed, then identify the next constraint.

```
1. How is the monthly report working for you?
2. What decisions has it helped you make?
3. What is still manual or awkward?
4. What is changing in the business next quarter?
5. Who else in the firm feels the same pain?
6. If we added [new service], what would that unlock?
7. What would make it a priority?
Close: "I will outline a small first step and you can decide."
```

### C6. Networking contact

**When to use:** you met at an event and agreed to a catch up.

**Instructions:** This is relationship building first. Be curious about their business, offer something useful, and only then move to need.

```
1. How has the year been in your business?
2. What are you focused on most right now?
3. What is slowing you down?
4. Who are your best customers, and how do you find more?
5. How can I be useful to you?
6. Do you know people who deal with [problem]?
Close: "I will send the article we talked about and keep you
in mind."
```

### C7. Multiple decision makers

**When to use:** more than one person must agree.

**Instructions:** Ask each person what success looks like to them. Request that all decision makers join the proposal review.

```
1. Who is involved in this decision?
2. What does each person need to see?
3. Who could say no, and why?
4. What happened with the last project like this?
5. What approvals are needed, and how long do they take?
6. Can everyone join a 20 minute review of the proposal?
Close: "I will tailor the proposal to each person's concern."
```

### C8. Burned by a previous provider

**When to use:** the buyer mentions a bad past experience.

**Instructions:** Do not criticise the other provider. Find out exactly what went wrong and how you will prevent it.

```
1. What happened last time?
2. At what point did you notice it was going wrong?
3. What should have been done differently?
4. What would you need to see to trust a new provider?
5. How could we reduce the risk (smaller first phase, weekly
   check ins, fixed scope)?
6. What does "done" look like for you?
Close: "Let me propose a smaller first phase so you can judge
the work before committing further."
```

### C9. Early stage "just exploring"

**When to use:** the buyer has little urgency and no budget yet.

**Instructions:** Help them clarify and do not push. Qualify the timeline honestly and schedule a future check in.

```
1. What got you looking at this?
2. What would need to happen for this to become a priority?
3. By when would you want a decision?
4. What is the budget process?
5. What would help you decide?
6. Would a short written overview help?
Close: "I will send the overview and check in on [date]."
```

### C10. Reactivated past client

**When to use:** someone you worked with before returns.

**Instructions:** Recap the previous result, then ask what changed. Update scope and price based on new facts.

```
1. Good to hear from you. What changed since we last worked
   together?
2. How did last time's work hold up?
3. What is the new priority?
4. Who is involved this time?
5. What constraints do you have on time and budget?
6. Should we start from the previous setup or reconsider it?
Close: "I will send an updated scope and price this week."
```

## After the call

Within one hour: update the CRM, write a three line summary in the buyer's own words, score using the worksheet in Chapter 11, and send a thank you with the agreed next step. Send the summary to the buyer; it confirms understanding and prevents drift.

```
Subject: Summary of today's call

Hi Ellis, thanks for your time. What I heard:
- Invoices go out up to 3 weeks late.
- Cash flow is tight at month end.
- You want this running before the year end.
Next step: I will send a proposal by Thursday.
Please tell me if I missed anything.
```

## Common mistakes and fixes

**Mistake:** Pitching in the first ten minutes.
**Fix:** Do not describe your service until you have summarised their problem back to them and they agreed.

**Mistake:** Skipping budget.
**Fix:** Ask once, plainly. If they refuse to answer, offer ranges: "Are we closer to A, B or C?"

**Mistake:** No next step.
**Fix:** Do not hang up without a dated action for both sides.

# Chapter 11. Client qualification

## Why qualify

Not every lead is a good client. Working with a poor fit costs time, margin and attention. Qualification is a structured judgement made after learning enough, and it protects both sides. You can say no politely and refer them elsewhere.

## Qualification worksheet

Score each factor from 0 to 2 (0 weak, 1 partial, 2 strong). Maximum 20.

```
CLIENT QUALIFICATION WORKSHEET

Lead: ______  Date: ______

1. Problem is real and named by them            (0-2)
2. Cost of the problem is clear                 (0-2)
3. Urgency: a reason to act within 90 days      (0-2)
4. Budget: range stated or confirmed            (0-2)
5. Authority: decision maker is on the call     (0-2)
6. Fit: matches my ICP and service              (0-2)
7. Scope is clear and realistic                 (0-2)
8. Communication: responsive, respectful        (0-2)
9. Payment: terms accepted in principle         (0-2)
10. Access: can provide inputs when needed      (0-2)

Total: ___ / 20
```

## How to read the score

| Score | Action |
|---|---|
| 16 to 20 | Send a proposal within 48 hours |
| 11 to 15 | Fix the weak factors first (ask, clarify, involve the decision maker) |
| 6 to 10 | Nurture; send resources; check in later |
| 0 to 5 | Politely decline and refer |

Two rules override the total. Any score of 0 on Authority or Budget means you cannot send a final proposal yet. A score of 0 on Communication or Payment means you pause, because these predict trouble later.

These cut offs are a decision aid I propose, not a validated statistical model. Adjust them after you review 20 of your own deals.

### Worked example (fictional)

Lead: Ridgeline Studio, director Ellis Wong.

| Factor | Score | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Problem | 2 | "Invoices wait weeks" |
| Cost | 1 | Cash flow mentioned, not quantified |
| Urgency | 2 | Year end in 8 weeks |
| Budget | 1 | Gave a monthly figure, not total |
| Authority | 2 | Ellis signs |
| Fit | 2 | In ICP |
| Scope | 1 | Unsure about second office |
| Communication | 2 | Replied same day |
| Payment | 1 | Wants quarterly invoicing |
| Access | 2 | Will share data on day one |

Total 16. Action: send a proposal within 48 hours. Open items before sending: quantify cost, confirm scope for the second office, agree payment terms.

## Red flags

- Refuses to say who decides.
- Asks for free sample work on their real project.
- Disputes price before knowing scope.
- Talks about previous providers with contempt and no self reflection.
- Wants a start date before agreeing terms.
- Cannot describe what success looks like.

A red flag is a question, not a verdict. Ask about it directly and judge the answer.

## Saying no well

```
Hi Ellis, thank you for the detail. After thinking it
through, I am not the right fit for this one because
[honest reason, e.g. the scope needs a specialist in
multi site tax]. I can recommend [name] who does that. If
things change, I am happy to talk again.
```

## Common mistakes and fixes

**Mistake:** Qualifying only on whether they can pay.
**Fix:** Use all ten factors; payment is one.

**Mistake:** Ignoring a low score because you need work.
**Fix:** Decide the minimum score in advance, when you are calm.

**Mistake:** Not updating the score.
**Fix:** Rescore after the proposal meeting.

# Chapter 12. Proposals and closing

## What a proposal is for

A proposal confirms what you heard, shows how you will solve it, states the price and terms, and makes the next step easy. If the call went well, nothing in the proposal should surprise the buyer. Forum discussions among freelancers repeatedly mention proposals that sit unopened in an inbox after a good call. The practical fix is to present the proposal live, or at least agree a date to discuss it, rather than emailing a document and hoping.

## Section by section structure (use for all frameworks)

1. **Summary of the situation** in the buyer's words.
2. **Goal and success measures.**
3. **Approach** in steps.
4. **Scope:** included and excluded.
5. **Timeline and milestones.**
6. **Investment:** price, payment schedule.
7. **Terms:** revisions, change requests, ownership, cancellation.
8. **Next steps:** what happens if they say yes.

## Five proposal frameworks

### P1. One page proposal

**When to use:** small projects, quick decisions.

**Instructions:** Keep it to one page; present in the call or follow with a short call.

```
Ridgeline Studio, billing setup. Prepared 12 Nov.

Situation: Invoices go out up to 3 weeks late; month end
takes a day for the director.
Goal: Invoices out within 5 days of a stage signing; one
profit report per project.
Approach: 1) Map stages. 2) Configure software. 3) Build the
report. 4) Train staff.
Included: setup, template, one training session, 30 days of
support. Not included: tax advice, data cleanup older than
12 months.
Timeline: 3 weeks from start date.
Investment: 1,800 fixed. 50% on acceptance, 50% on delivery.
To start: reply "yes" and I will send the agreement.
```

### P2. Three option proposal

**When to use:** the buyer is price sensitive or unsure about scope.

**Instructions:** Offer three levels. The middle one should be the one you want; the others make choice easy.

```
Option A, Essentials: Setup and report. 1,800.
Option B, Recommended: Setup, report, 3 months of monthly
 close with review call. 3,600.
Option C, Full: Option B plus quarterly planning and
 year end preparation. 5,400.
All options: 3 week setup. Payment: 50% on acceptance,
balance on delivery (monthly services billed monthly).
Differences: B adds ongoing close; C adds planning.
```

### P3. Problem, solution, proof proposal

**When to use:** the buyer must convince others, so the logic needs to be clear.

**Instructions:** Lead with the problem and its cost, then the solution, then proof, then price.

```
Problem: Late invoices delay cash by about 3 weeks, and
reporting takes 8 hours a month.
Cost: At 8 hours at 60 an hour, 480 a month in time alone,
before cash flow effects (the director's own estimate).
Solution: Stage billing, a weekly invoice day, a project
profit report.
Proof: Two similar studios completed this setup (case notes
available on request).
Price: 1,800 setup, 600 a month for monthly close.
Decision needed by: 20 Nov to start before year end.
```

### P4. Phased proposal with paid first step

**When to use:** high uncertainty, larger scope, or a nervous buyer.

**Instructions:** Price phase one as a standalone deliverable. The buyer decides on phase two with evidence.

```
Phase 1, Diagnostic (2 weeks): Review current billing,
deliver a findings report and plan. 900.
Phase 2, Build (3 weeks): Implement the plan. Price confirmed
after Phase 1, estimated 1,800 to 2,400.
Phase 3, Run (monthly): 600 a month, 3 month minimum.
You may stop after any phase. Phase 1 fee is credited
against Phase 2 if you continue within 30 days.
```

### P5. Retainer proposal

**When to use:** ongoing work with a steady, recurring need.

**Instructions:** Define hours or deliverables per month, response times, reporting and how to end it.

```
Monthly retainer: bookkeeping and reporting
Includes: month end close, project profit report, one 45 minute
review call, email questions answered within 2 working days.
Not included: tax filings, payroll.
Fee: 600 a month, invoiced on the 1st, payable in 14 days.
Term: 3 months, then rolling with 30 days notice.
Review: quarterly, to adjust scope and fee.
```

## Closing the conversation

Present the proposal live where possible. Walk through the situation, the approach and the price, then ask a direct question.

```
Does this match what you need? Is there anything you would
want changed before we go ahead? If not, shall we start on
Monday 1 December?
```

Silence after a proposal is usually not a no; it is often inertia. Chapter 14 covers follow up.

## Agreement and start

Once they accept: send a short agreement (a lawyer can review your standard template), an invoice for the deposit, and an onboarding checklist. Clarity at the start prevents disputes later. This book does not provide contract wording; get a template reviewed for your jurisdiction.

## Common mistakes and fixes

**Mistake:** Sending a long document by email after the call.
**Fix:** Offer a 20 minute walkthrough, or send a one page version.

**Mistake:** Hiding the price at the end.
**Fix:** State it plainly, tied to scope and options.

**Mistake:** No deadline or next step.
**Fix:** Say what happens on acceptance and by when you hold the start slot.
