# Chapter 13. Pricing and negotiation

## Four pricing models

| Model | You charge for | Works best when | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly | Time spent | Scope is unclear or changes | Rewards slowness, caps income at hours worked |
| Project | A defined deliverable | Scope is clear | Underestimating effort |
| Value | A share of the benefit | Result is measurable and big | Hard to prove; needs trust |
| Retainer | Ongoing availability or output | Recurring need | Scope creep inside the monthly fee |

This book does not state market rates. They vary by country, niche, experience and demand, and any figure I gave would be unsourced. Use your own costs and your own quotes. All numbers below are arithmetic examples in an unnamed currency, and I ran them in a script to confirm them.

## Step 1: find your minimum hourly rate

Start from the income you need, add business costs, and divide by hours you can actually bill.

```
Income target (after business costs):  60,000
Business costs per year:               12,000
Weeks worked per year:                 46
Billable hours per week:               25
Billable hours per year:               46 x 25 = 1,150
Minimum rate: (60,000 + 12,000) / 1,150 = 62.61
```

The 25 billable hours assume the rest of the week goes to acquisition, admin and learning, which is why Chapter 15 gives it a budget. If you bill 35 hours a week the rate falls; if you bill 15 it rises. This is a floor, not a price. Tax, benefits and savings depend on your country and are not modelled here.

## Hourly

Rate times hours. Use it when scope is truly unknown, and cap it with an estimate: "up to 20 hours, I will tell you at 15."

## Project

Estimate hours, multiply by your floor, add a buffer for uncertainty.

```
Estimated hours:        40
Base: 40 x 62.61 =      2,504.35
Buffer 20%:             2,504.35 x 1.20 = 3,005.22
Quoted (rounded):       3,000
```

Check against reality: if the job takes 52 hours, you earned 3,000 / 52 = 57.69 per hour, below your floor, while the client paid 3,000 for a result. At 52 hours your floor would have required 52 x 62.61 = 3,255.65. Track actual hours on every project to improve the next estimate. A project fee also lets you gain if you work faster.

## Value pricing

Price against the benefit the client expects, if you can credibly estimate it with them.

```
Client expects extra yearly profit:   30,000 (their estimate)
Fee at 10% of that:                   3,000
Fee at 15% of that:                   4,500
```

Use the buyer's own estimate, not yours, and write the assumption into the proposal. Never promise the benefit. A value based fee only makes sense when your work plausibly contributes and the client accepts the logic. Treat percentages as a negotiation starting point, not a rule.

## Retainer

```
Monthly hours included:      10
Hours x floor:               10 x 62.61 = 626.09
Availability premium 15%:    626.09 x 1.15 = 720.00
Monthly fee:                 720
```

The premium pays for reserved capacity and for the client's guaranteed access. A retainer gives you predictable income and gives the client priority; define what is included and what is billed extra.

## Present a price

1. State the price once, clearly, after confirming scope.
2. Tie it to an outcome and a timeline, not to hours.
3. Offer options so the choice is which, not whether.
4. Stay quiet after you say it.

## Negotiation

Negotiation is trading, not conceding. When a buyer asks for a discount, change the scope or the terms in exchange.

```
Request: "Can you do it for 2,700 instead of 3,000?"
Reply: "I can do 2,700 if we remove the training session
and handle the revisions in one round. Would that work?"
```

A discount of 10% on 3,000 is 300. At your floor of 62.61, that is 300 / 62.61 = 4.79 hours of work given away. Expressing a discount in hours makes its real cost clear.

Other tradeable items: payment up front, a longer commitment, a case study right, a faster decision date, a referral. Do not trade price for nothing.

## Handling "too expensive"

- Ask: "Too expensive compared to what?"
- Revisit the cost of the problem the buyer described.
- Offer a smaller first phase (P4 in Chapter 12).
- Accept that some buyers will not pay your price. That is acceptable.

## Payment terms

State them in the proposal: deposit, schedule, due date, late fees only if your jurisdiction and agreement allow. Many freelancers take a deposit before work begins. Get a template reviewed for your location.

## Common mistakes and fixes

**Mistake:** Setting prices from competitors' public rates.
**Fix:** Start from your costs and outcomes; use competitors only as context.

**Mistake:** Discounting without exchange.
**Fix:** Every price drop buys something: scope, speed, commitment or payment terms.

**Mistake:** Fixed price with no change process.
**Fix:** Write what counts as a change and how it is priced.

# Chapter 14. Follow up systems

## Why follow up

People are busy. A single message is easy to miss, and one reason forum discussions give for non replies is that the reply comes after a nudge, not after the first note. That is anecdotal; your own tracking is the proof. Follow up is not nagging if each message adds something and has an end.

## Follow up rules

1. Every lead has a next action and a date. Nothing sits without both.
2. Cold outreach: at most four touches (first message plus three), spaced 3 to 7 days apart, then stop and note the date. Do not send more.
3. Each follow up adds something new: a fact, a resource, a clearer question.
4. Opt outs stop the sequence immediately and permanently.
5. After a proposal, follow up on a schedule: day 2, day 7, day 14.
6. A clear "no" is a result. Thank them and close the record.

## Ten follow up templates

### F1. First gentle nudge

**When to use:** 3 to 4 days after a cold email with no reply.

**Instructions:** Keep it to two lines and restate the question.

```
Subject: Re: Your new Bath office

Hi Ellis, a quick nudge in case this got buried. Is project
billing something you are looking at this quarter?

Tom
```

### F2. Value add follow up

**When to use:** 7 days after the first message.

**Instructions:** Give something useful that needs no reply.

```
Hi Ellis, I put together a one page checklist for stage
billing in studios. It is attached; take it whether or not we
talk. If you want to go through it together, say so.

Tom
```

### F3. Breakup message

**When to use:** after three touches without reply.

**Instructions:** Close the loop politely and leave the door open.

```
Hi Ellis, I will assume the timing is not right and stop
writing. If invoice delays become a priority, you are welcome
to contact me. Best wishes with the Bath office.

Tom
```

### F4. After call, before proposal

**When to use:** within an hour of a discovery call.

**Instructions:** Summarise in their words and confirm the next step.

```
Hi Ellis, thank you for today. What I heard: invoices wait up
to three weeks, the year end is in eight weeks, and you want
one profit report per project. I will send the proposal by
Thursday 5 pm. Please correct anything I missed.

Tom
```

### F5. Two days after proposal

**When to use:** day 2 after sending.

**Instructions:** Ask a question that moves things forward, not "just checking in".

```
Hi Ellis, did the proposal answer your questions? If useful, I
can walk you through it in 15 minutes this week. Tuesday or
Wednesday?

Tom
```

### F6. Seven days after proposal

**When to use:** day 7 with no reply.

**Instructions:** Offer to adjust and ask what is blocking.

```
Hi Ellis, I know year end is busy. If price or scope is
the sticking point, I can adjust the scope to fit. What
would help you decide?

Tom
```

### F7. Revive a silent proposal

**When to use:** 14 days or more of silence.

**Instructions:** Give a reason to act and an easy way to decline.

```
Hi Ellis, I can hold the 1 December start date until Friday.
After that my next slot is in January. A quick "yes", "not
now" or "no" is fine, so I can plan.

Tom
```

### F8. Verbal yes, awaiting signature

**When to use:** the buyer agreed verbally but has not signed.

**Instructions:** Make the next step simple and remove obstacles.

```
Hi Ellis, great news that we are going ahead. To hold the
start date I need the signed agreement and the deposit
invoice paid by Wednesday. Is anything holding that up?

Tom
```

### F9. After a no

**When to use:** the buyer declined.

**Instructions:** Thank them, ask one learning question, and keep the relationship.

```
Hi Ellis, thank you for letting me know. If you are willing,
what was the main reason (price, timing, fit)? It helps me
improve. Good luck with the year end.

Tom
```

### F10. Scheduled resurface after "not now"

**When to use:** on the date the buyer asked you to return.

**Instructions:** Remind them of the earlier conversation and ask if the situation changed.

```
Hi Ellis, in September you said to check back after the Bath
office opened. Is project billing back on the agenda?

Tom
```

## Lead tracking system

### Stages

| Stage | Definition | Exit rule |
|---|---|---|
| New | Qualified on ICP, not contacted | First message sent |
| Contacted | First message sent | Reply, or 4 touches done |
| Replied | They responded | Call booked, or declined |
| Call booked | Date set | Call held or no show |
| Call held | Discovery done | Scored; proposal sent or declined |
| Proposal sent | Proposal delivered | Won, lost or nurture |
| Won | Signed and paid deposit | Move to client delivery |
| Lost | Declined or no response after follow up | Record reason |
| Nurture | Not now; dated follow up | Revisit on date |

### Columns

Name, company, role, source, trigger, stage, date contacted, date replied, date call held, date proposal sent, date won, deal value, next action, next action date, fit score, notes. Appendix B gives the spreadsheet layout.

### Rules

- Update the sheet the same day.
- No lead without a next action date.
- A lead untouched for 14 days is flagged: act or close.
- Record a reason for every loss.
- Keep a do not contact list, and check every new list against it.

### Weekly review routine (30 minutes, Friday)

1. Count contacts, replies, calls, proposals and wins this week.
2. List overdue next actions and clear them.
3. Review lost deals for reasons.
4. Pick the weakest conversion step and change one thing.
5. Plan next week's volume.

## Common mistakes and fixes

**Mistake:** Following up with "just checking in".
**Fix:** Add something new or ask a specific question.

**Mistake:** No end to the sequence.
**Fix:** Four touches, then a dated pause.

**Mistake:** Following up only on proposals.
**Fix:** Track every stage; the leak is often earlier.

# Chapter 15. Building repeatable client acquisition processes

## A weekly rhythm

Consistency matters more than intensity. A repeatable week looks like this (adapt to your hours):

| Day | Block | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Research and add 10 leads | 45 min |
| Tuesday | Send 10 personal messages | 45 min |
| Wednesday | Content post and community replies | 40 min |
| Thursday | Follow ups and calls | 60 min |
| Friday | Proposals, CRM and weekly review | 45 min |

That is about 3 hours 55 minutes a week, roughly 47 minutes a day over five days. Check this against the pricing model: at 25 billable hours a week plus under 5 for acquisition, the week fits into 30 hours (arithmetic: 25 + 3.9 = 28.9).

## Measure four numbers

1. Contacts per week.
2. Reply rate (replies / contacts).
3. Calls held per week.
4. Win rate (won / proposals).

Change one variable at a time. If replies are low, change the first line. If calls are few, change the ask. If proposals are lost, change qualification or pricing.

## 30 day roadmap

Time budget: about 60 minutes a day, five days a week, plus a 30 minute review each Friday.

### Week 1: foundation

| Day | Task |
|---|---|
| 1 | Fill in the ICP worksheet (Chapter 3) |
| 2 | Write your offer statement (Chapter 4) |
| 3 | Build one proof item (Chapter 5) |
| 4 | List 30 warm contacts |
| 5 | Set up the CRM sheet (Appendix B) and review |

### Week 2: warm outreach

| Day | Task |
|---|---|
| 6 | Send 5 messages from O1, O4, O6 |
| 7 | Ask 3 contacts for introductions (O2) |
| 8 | Add 15 researched leads |
| 9 | Follow up on week 1 messages |
| 10 | Book calls; weekly review |

### Week 3: cold outreach

| Day | Task |
|---|---|
| 11 | Send 10 emails (O7 to O14), with opt out |
| 12 | Send 5 LinkedIn notes by hand (O15) |
| 13 | Publish one post (Chapter 9) |
| 14 | Follow ups F1, F2 |
| 15 | Hold calls; score each; weekly review |

### Week 4: calls and proposals

| Day | Task |
|---|---|
| 16 | Send 10 more messages |
| 17 | Hold discovery calls using C1 to C10 |
| 18 | Send proposals to qualified leads |
| 19 | Follow up on proposals (F5, F6) |
| 20 | Full review: conversion rates, weak step, plan month 2 |

Days 21 to 30 repeat weeks 3 and 4 with changes based on the review. I cannot say how many clients this will produce; that depends on your niche, offer and market. What the 30 days give you is a tested process and real numbers.

## Systemise

- Save templates in a folder; improve the best performer.
- Create a checklist for each stage.
- Block calendar time for acquisition and treat it as client work.
- Review the system every quarter: drop what does not work.

## Common mistakes and fixes

**Mistake:** Stopping acquisition when you are busy.
**Fix:** Keep a minimum (five messages a week).

**Mistake:** Changing everything at once.
**Fix:** One change per two weeks.

**Mistake:** No review.
**Fix:** The Friday review is the system.

# Conclusion

A client acquisition system is a small set of repeated habits: a clear target, an honest offer, proof, contact with real people, good conversations, clear proposals, fair prices and follow up. None of it needs cleverness. It needs a calendar, a spreadsheet and the discipline to look at the numbers.
